How to Check If a Website Is Safe Before You Trust It

Before you enter a password or card on a website, run these quick checks: the exact domain, the connection, the age and reputation of the site, and its contact details. Here is the full checklist.

To check if a website is safe, verify four things before you trust it: the exact domain, the connection, the site’s age and reputation, and its real contact details. If any of these is wrong, or the page pressures you to log in or pay urgently, stop. Here is the full checklist.

1. Read the domain, carefully, from the start

The domain is the most reliable signal, and the most faked. Scammers register lookalikes that pass a quick glance:

  • Extra words: paytm-verify.com, sbi-kyc-update.info. The real brand is buried, not at the root.
  • Swapped characters: arnazon.com, g00gle.com, flipkart-in.shop.
  • Wrong endings: a bank on .info, .shop, or .xyz instead of its official domain.

Read the address from the beginning to the first single slash, and ask: is the part right before .com (or .in) exactly the real brand? If not, leave.

2. Check the connection, but do not rely on it

Look for https:// and the padlock. This means traffic is encrypted, which is good. But HTTPS does not mean the site is honest. Most phishing sites now use HTTPS too, because certificates are free. The padlock is the floor, not the ceiling. A missing padlock is a clear no, but a present padlock proves very little.

3. Check the site’s age and reputation

Genuine businesses leave a trail. Scam sites usually do not.

  • Domain age. A domain registered days or weeks ago, on a site asking for money or logins, is a major red flag. A domain age lookup shows the registration date.
  • Reviews and mentions. Search the site’s name with words like “scam” or “review.” Real businesses have history; fresh scam sites have none, or a sudden burst of complaints.
  • Consistency. Broken links, mismatched branding, stock text, and no social presence all point to a hastily built trap.

4. Look for real contact and policy details

Legitimate sites tell you who they are: a physical address, a working support channel, clear return and privacy policies. Scam sites often have a lone contact form, a free email address, or policies copied from elsewhere. If you cannot find who stands behind the site, do not hand it your money.

Red flags that should stop you immediately

  • Urgent pressure to log in, pay, or “verify” within minutes.
  • A request for an OTP, PIN, or full card details in an unusual place.
  • A deal that is far too good to be true.
  • A download or app install to “continue,” especially an APK file.
  • You arrived from a suspicious SMS or email link rather than typing the address yourself.

If you already entered details

If you typed a password or card number on a site you now doubt, act quickly: change that password from a trusted device, contact the real company through an official number, watch your accounts, and if money moved, report it fast. The steps are in our guide on what to do after clicking a phishing link.

Checking a website takes under a minute once you know what to look at, and it is the minute that protects everything behind your passwords.

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell if a website is safe? Check that the domain is spelled exactly right, that the connection uses HTTPS, that the site is not brand new, and that it has real contact details and reviews. If anything is off, or the site pressures you to pay or log in urgently, do not enter your details.

Does HTTPS mean a website is safe? No. HTTPS only means the connection is encrypted, not that the site is honest. Many scam and phishing sites also use HTTPS. Treat the padlock as the bare minimum, not proof of trust.

How do I check who owns a website or how old it is? A domain age or WHOIS lookup shows when a domain was registered. Scam sites are often only days or weeks old. A very recent registration on a site asking for money or logins is a strong warning sign.

What is the safest way to check a suspicious link? Do not open it directly. Use a dedicated link-reputation service, or forward it to Kaval on WhatsApp so you can review the link and its context before you visit the page. No automated response proves that a link is safe.
