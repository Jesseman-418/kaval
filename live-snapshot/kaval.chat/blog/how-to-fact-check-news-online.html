How to Fact Check News Online: Methods That Actually Work

Learn how to fact-check news articles, viral claims, and social media posts using free tools and trusted sources. A practical guide to spotting misinformation in under a minute.

You can fact-check many claims quickly using the right tools. One route is to send the claim to Kaval on WhatsApp, which can compare available reporting and explain the evidence and uncertainty when relevant checks are available. For manual verification, cross-reference wire agencies and reverse image search any photo. Here are the methods that actually work.

1. Check the source

Before sharing or believing any claim, check where it came from.

  • Look at the domain. Sites like reuters.com and apnews.com are wire agencies with editorial standards. Unusual domains (extra hyphens, misspelled names, .info endings) often signal unreliable sources.
  • Check the About page. Legitimate outlets identify their editors, ownership, and contact information. Missing or vague About pages are a red flag.
  • Search the author. Does the journalist exist, and do they have a track record of credible reporting?

2. Read laterally

Professional fact-checkers do not stay on the page making a claim. They open new tabs and ask what other, independent sources say about the same claim and about the site itself. Instead of judging an article by how convincing it looks, judge it by what the rest of the credible web says. If only one obscure site is carrying a dramatic story, that is a signal, not a scoop.

3. Cross-reference with wire agencies

Wire agencies like Reuters, AP News, and AFP are the original sources for most breaking news. If a major event happened, these agencies will have reported on it. Search the claim on at least two of:

  • Reuters
  • AP News
  • BBC News

If none of them are covering a story that claims to be major breaking news, that is a strong signal something is off.

4. Reverse image search

Viral images are frequently taken out of context. A photo from a 2019 protest might be recaptioned as a 2026 event. To check an image:

  1. Right-click the image and select “Search image with Google,” or use Google Lens.
  2. Look at when and where the image first appeared online.
  3. Compare captions across different uses of the same image.

If the image predates the claimed event, it is being used out of context.

5. Check the date and context

Old news resurfaces constantly, sometimes by accident and sometimes to mislead. Before reacting, confirm the date of the article and the event, and check whether a headline matches the actual content. A real story from three years ago, reshared as if it happened today, is a classic way misinformation spreads without anyone technically lying.

6. Use dedicated fact-checking tools

Several free tools exist specifically for verification:

  • Kaval: a WhatsApp-first assistant that can compare claims with available sources, dates, and context. It can also help discuss image-manipulation signals and suspicious URLs; the exact checks depend on the item.
  • Google Fact Check Explorer: searches fact-checks published by verified organizations worldwide.
  • Snopes: one of the longest-running fact-checking sites covering viral claims and urban legends.

Our roundup of the best free fact-checking tools and websites compares more options. The advantage of a focused assistant is reducing the work: send a claim and get a review of the available evidence instead of searching several sites by hand.

7. Watch for AI-generated content

With generative AI, synthetic images, videos, and articles are increasingly common. Look for:

  • Images: unnatural skin texture, warped backgrounds, inconsistent lighting, distorted hands.
  • Videos: lip-sync issues, flickering around face edges, unnatural eye movement.
  • Text: generic phrasing and confident factual claims with no specific sources.

Detection tools can flag synthetic content more reliably than the eye. Our guide on how to detect AI-generated images and deepfakes goes deeper.

The emotional-manipulation check

One habit is worth more than any tool: notice your own reaction. Misinformation is engineered to make you feel something strong, outrage, fear, vindication, so you share before you think. If a claim makes you want to forward it immediately, that urge is precisely the moment to slow down and verify. The strongest emotional pull often marks the weakest evidence.

Quick checklist

Before sharing any news or claim, run through this:

  1. Is the source credible and identifiable?
  2. Do independent, credible sources say the same thing?
  3. Is the image original and in the right context?
  4. Is the date current, and does the headline match the story?
  5. Does a fact-checking tool confirm or deny the claim?
  6. Could the image or video be AI-generated?

If a claim fails two or more of these checks, do not share it.

The bottom line

Fact-checking does not have to be time-consuming. A short pause can stop you from spreading misinformation to hundreds of people.
