Kaval vs ChatGPT for Fact-Checking: Which Is Better in 2026?

Kaval vs ChatGPT for fact-checking: compare source review, explanations, image and link checks, and the WhatsApp workflow for suspicious claims.

Kaval and ChatGPT solve different problems. Kaval is a WhatsApp-first review workflow: send a claim, message, image, link, or document and it can investigate relevant evidence when the needed checks are available, explain uncertainty, and suggest next steps. ChatGPT is a general-purpose assistant that can reason about the same material and, depending on its current tools and setup, may also browse or inspect files. Neither is a substitute for the original source or a dedicated security check. For a suspicious message, Kaval’s advantage is the handoff in the app where the message arrived; for broad explanation and analysis, ChatGPT may be the better fit.

That said, it’s not quite that simple. They’re different tools built for different jobs. Here’s where each one actually makes sense.

What Each Tool Actually Does

Kaval

Kaval is a WhatsApp contact for practical review. You send a claim, link, image, document, or message and it can investigate the relevant context and sources when those checks are available.

The evidence matters more than a bare label. When sources are used, Kaval can explain where the information came from, what it supports, and what remains uncertain. It explains the available evidence rather than replacing a check against the original source, so important claims still deserve that check.

Beyond text claims, it handles things most fact-checking tools don’t:

  • Image review: send an image for context and signals worth checking. No automated review can prove that an image is synthetic or authentic. More on this in our deepfake detection guide.
  • Suspicious-link review: send a link or the message around it for a contextual review when relevant checks are available. Use a dedicated reputation service as a second check; our link safety guide explains the layers.
  • Data-exposure guidance: ask what to do after a possible breach, and use the official breach-notification route where appropriate. Our email breach guide explains the workflow.

It also works on WhatsApp. Forward a suspicious message to +91 7200218310 and ask for a review without leaving the conversation. The response should be treated as useful guidance, not proof that an item is safe or true.

ChatGPT

ChatGPT is a general-purpose AI chatbot from OpenAI. It’s trained on a massive dataset and can discuss, analyze, and reason about almost anything. People use it for writing, coding, research, brainstorming, and yes, sometimes for checking if something is true.

The problem is it wasn’t designed for verification. When you ask ChatGPT to fact-check something:

  • Its access to live sources, browsing, and file or image tools depends on the current product and setup.
  • It can produce inaccurate or uncited claims, so check important details against the original source.
  • A discussion of a suspicious URL is not a security scan, and an image description is not proof of authenticity.
  • It does not replace an official breach-notification service or a dedicated security tool.
  • A polished answer is not proof.

ChatGPT can absolutely help you think through whether a claim seems plausible. But “seems plausible based on what I know” is a different thing from an answer that clearly shows the evidence and its limits.

Where Kaval Wins

Viral WhatsApp Forwards

Your family group chat lights up with a message claiming some new government scheme or health scare. With Kaval, you forward that message directly to the WhatsApp contact and ask for a review. Kaval can explain the available evidence and uncertainty right inside the app where you got it.

With ChatGPT, you may need to leave the conversation and recreate the context. Whichever tool you use, check important claims against the original source before forwarding them.

Suspicious Links

Someone sends you a link that promises a free iPhone or claims your bank account is locked. Do you click it?

Kaval can review the URL and surrounding message for suspicious signals when the relevant checks are available, but that does not establish that the link is safe. ChatGPT’s current browsing and tool setup varies, and a conversational answer is not a security scan. Do not open the link or enter credentials based on either response alone. More on layered checks in our link safety guide.

Deepfake Images

A photo goes viral claiming to show a political figure in a compromising situation. Real or AI-generated?

Kaval can review the image and point out context or signals worth investigating, but no automated response can prove whether an image is synthetic. ChatGPT can also discuss what it sees, but neither tool establishes authenticity alone. We go deeper on the techniques in our deepfake detection guide.

Data Breach Checks

You hear about a massive breach at a service you use. Is your email in it? Start with the service’s notice or an official public breach-notification route. Kaval can help interpret the context and next steps when you send it a message; ChatGPT can explain recovery steps, but neither replaces the underlying notification service. See our breach checking guide.

Where ChatGPT Wins

ChatGPT is genuinely better at several things, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

It’s great at open-ended research. Want to understand how carbon credits work, or the history behind a geopolitical conflict? ChatGPT can synthesize that and explain it at whatever depth you need. Kaval can also help interpret a specific item, but it is optimized for practical, message-sized help rather than a full research workspace.

It’s also often better for long-form analysis. Paste in a 5,000-word article and ask it to find the claims, spot logical gaps, or evaluate the argument. Kaval is better suited to a focused question and practical follow-through.

And look, ChatGPT does a hundred things that have nothing to do with fact-checking. Brainstorming, writing, coding, general knowledge (“How does CRISPR work?”). If you’re comparing them strictly as fact-checking tools, that’s not relevant. But it’s worth noting that ChatGPT earns its place on your phone for reasons that go well beyond verification.

When to Use Each Tool

The short version: use the tool that was built for what you’re doing.

Use Kaval when you need to:

  • Review a specific claim, headline, message, link, image, or document
  • Ask what the available evidence supports and what remains uncertain
  • Review a WhatsApp forward without leaving the app
  • Get practical next steps before you share, click, or act

Use ChatGPT when you need to:

  • Understand a complex topic in depth
  • Analyze a long document or article
  • Get background context on something before you verify specific claims
  • Brainstorm, write, or explore ideas

Honestly, using both together is a solid approach. Use ChatGPT to understand the landscape around a topic, then use Kaval to verify the specific claims that matter. Our fact-checking guide covers a full verification workflow that fits this kind of two-tool approach.

FAQ

Can ChatGPT replace dedicated fact-checkers?

No single chatbot replaces a fact-checker or primary source. ChatGPT can produce plausible but incorrect or uncited information, and its live tools vary. It is useful for reasoning about claims, but treating its output as verified fact is risky. Dedicated services, editorial fact-checkers, and a focused Kaval review can each contribute different evidence; compare them rather than trusting a label alone. See our tools roundup.
